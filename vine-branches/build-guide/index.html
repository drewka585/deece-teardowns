Building the page that has to do the selling

You are building this one yourself, so here is everything I have that would help. A conversion playbook you can load straight into your AI assistant, a free way to get the first version built without writing code, and the order to do it in.

The one rule
worth knowing

Everyone who lands on this page has been reading your daily messages for weeks. That single fact changes most of the standard advice.

Nearly every landing page guide is written for cold traffic, someone who clicked an ad and has never heard of you. That reader needs a long page, because they have to be convinced from zero. Your reader does not. They already know what the daily message is worth, because they have been opening it every morning.

So the page is short, it names only what Tier 2 adds on top of what they already have, and it states the price without making them hunt. Padding it out with things they already know reads as a pitch, and a pitch is the one thing that can cost you the trust the messages have been building.

The playbook below is built on that translation. It came out of about forty lessons of paid-traffic conversion training, and I have already done the work of separating what carries over from what does not.

Two

Get the first version built for free

Google AI Studio will build the page from a description. No code, no cost.

The fastest way to a real page is to let Google build the frame and then do the actual work on the copy. AI Studio is free to use and it will hand you the finished files, which is the part that matters. A tool that builds you a page you cannot export is a trap.

  1. Open the builder

    Go to aistudio.google.com/apps and sign in with a Google account. There is a prompt box in the middle of the screen.

  2. Ask for a single file, not an app

    This matters more than it sounds. Left alone the builder will produce a full application with a server behind it, which is a real hosting problem you do not need. Ask for one self-contained page and you get something you can put anywhere.

    Paste this

    Build a single self-contained HTML file. All CSS and any JavaScript inline in
that one file. No React, no build step, no backend, no server, no external
libraries. It must open correctly by double-clicking the file.

It is a landing page for a paid tier of a daily scripture study service. The
reader already receives a free daily message and is deciding whether to start
a paid trial. Warm audience, quiet tone, no hype, no countdown timers.

Sections, in this order: a headline and one-sentence subheadline, a short block
naming what the paid tier adds, the price stated plainly, a proof section, a
short section on who it is not for, and one call-to-action button that repeats
near the top and at the bottom.

Use placeholder text throughout. I am replacing all of the words myself. Get
the structure, spacing and typography right and leave the copy for me.

It must work on a phone. Most readers will open it from a message.

    Let it build, look at the preview on the right, and ask for changes in plain language until the shape is right. Say things like make the headline larger, or move the price above the proof section.

  3. Take the files

    Use the export option and choose the zip download. You can also connect a GitHub account and push it there, which is worth doing if you want a record of every version.

    You now have an HTML file. That is the whole page.

Three

Do the real work in Claude

The builder gets you a shape. The words are what converts, and this is where the playbook earns its keep.

Open a new chat, or the project you made in step one, and upload the HTML file. Then work through it in this order. Do not skip to the design, and do not write the headline first.

  1. Write the confirmation page before the landing page

    This is the one nobody builds and it is worth more than most of what goes on the page itself. Attention peaks at the exact moment someone starts the trial and decays from there. A default thanks-you-are-in screen wastes it. In the source material, work on that page alone moved a show rate from around sixty percent past eighty-five.

    Paste this

    Using the doctrine, write the confirmation page a member sees immediately after
starting the Tier 2 trial. Answer the questions people actually ask right after
signing up, say plainly when the first charge lands and how to cancel, and give
them something to read or do right now. It is not a thank-you screen.

  2. Write the headline and the price block

    Ask for options, not one answer. Then pick.

    Paste this

    Write eight headline and subheadline pairs for this page, each running the
three-step check from the doctrine. Vary the angle across them rather than
rewording one idea. Then, under each, tell me in one line what desire it is
tapping and what it is assuming the reader already believes.

After that, write the price block. Price visible without scrolling, what
happens when the trial ends stated right next to the offer, including the
charge date and how to cancel.

    Give it the exact wording of the message that links to the page. The headline has to echo that promise in the same words, or the mismatch reads as bait and they leave before the first paragraph.

  3. Write the rest, then have it grade its own work

    Sections in order: what Tier 2 adds, proof, who it is not for. Then run the check.

    Paste this

    Now run the full ship checklist against this page and show me every single item
that fails, with the fix. Be hard on it. Do not tell me it passes when it does
not, and do not soften anything.

    On proof: you will not have testimonials at launch, and you should not invent them. Show a real sample of the Tier 2 material so people can judge it themselves, say plainly that they are early, and make cancellation obvious. Being honest about being new is itself the proof of the transparency the rest of the page claims.

  4. Then the design

    Last, not first. Ask it to tighten spacing and typography, check that it holds up on a narrow phone screen, and confirm the button is reachable without scrolling on a small screen.

Four

Two things that make it plug into the build

Small, but the second one is easy to lose and expensive to add back.

The link lives in a settings record. I am keeping the page address in a settings field rather than hardcoded in the automation, so you can change the page, the wording or the whole address later without anyone touching the build. Nothing for you to do here, it just means you are never blocked on me.

The link carries the member ID and your page has to keep it. The address will arrive looking something like yoursite.com/tier2?m=abc123. That tail is how a signup gets tied back to a person. If the page drops it, or a form submit loses it, we get conversions with no way to tell whose they were, and the Tier 2 invitation fires off real engagement rather than a fixed day count, so that link matters.

Give this to Claude with the page

The page will be opened with a query parameter on the URL, like ?m=abc123.
Read that value on load, keep it through the whole page, and pass it along
when the visitor starts the trial, including through any redirect to the
payment processor. It must survive every navigation on the page.

Five

Before it goes live

The short version of the checklist inside the download.

  • The headline says the same thing, in the same words, as the message that links to it.
  • The price is visible without scrolling.
  • The charge date and how to cancel sit next to the trial offer.
  • Exactly one action on the page, with nothing competing for the click.
  • The button says what happens next, not Get started.
  • Nothing gates the button. No email capture in front of it, no timer, no scroll requirement.
  • Who it is not for appears near the button.
  • Proof sits immediately before the final call to action, not at the top.
  • No countdowns, no spots remaining, no exclamation points.
  • Read out loud, every sentence would sound at home inside a 6am message.
  • Footer has privacy, terms and real contact details.
  • Loads in under five seconds. Thirty-seven percent leave after five.
  • The page keeps whatever is on the end of the URL.
  • The confirmation page exists and is not the payment processor's default screen.

Six

One thing I would push on

Do not build this as a page floating on its own. Milestone 5 already needs a checkout surface, because a chat thread cannot take a card, so there is a page in this build either way.

If yours is the front of that same site, someone reads your explanation and lands on checkout without ever leaving. Same site, one less seam, and nothing you build now gets thrown away. Whatever you use to put it online, keep it somewhere we can point the checkout at later.
